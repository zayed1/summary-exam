1 The Magnificent Seven slides 3–10

To meet or exceed customer expectations, a product should be produced by a process that is stable / repeatable. SPC can be applied to any process; its seven major tools — “the magnificent seven” — are:

#ToolPurpose
1Histogram / stem-and-leaf plotShow the shape and spread of the data.
2Check sheetOrganized collection of defect data over time.
3Pareto chartRank defects in descending order to find the "vital few."
4Cause-and-effect diagramFind the root causes of a problem (fishbone).
5Defect concentration diagramMark where defects occur on the product.
6Scatter diagramStudy the relationship between two variables.
7Control chartMonitor a process over time and detect out-of-control conditions.
⚠ Note

Of the seven, the Shewhart control chart is probably the most technically sophisticated.

Constructing a Cause-and-Effect Diagram slide 8

  1. Define the problem or effect to be analyzed.
  2. Form the team to perform the analysis (often via brainstorming).
  3. Draw the effect box and the center line.
  4. Specify the major potential cause categories and join them as boxes to the center line.
  5. Identify the possible causes and classify them into the categories; create new categories if necessary.
  6. Rank-order the causes to identify those most likely to impact the problem.
  7. Take corrective action.

2 Chance & Assignable Causes of Variation slides 21–24

Chance causes

A certain amount of inherent / natural variability always exists. This natural variability (“background noise”) is the cumulative effect of many small, essentially unavoidable causes — a “stable system of chance causes.”

✔ In statistical control

A process operating with only chance causes of variation present is said to be in statistical control.

Assignable causes

Other variability arises mainly from three sources: (1) improperly adjusted or controlled machines, (2) operator errors, (3) defective raw material. This variability is large compared to the background noise and represents an unacceptable level of performance.

✘ Out of control

A process operating in the presence of assignable causes is said to be out of control.

🎯 Objective of SPC

The major objective is to quickly detect the occurrence of assignable causes. The control chart is an on-line monitoring technique; it can also estimate process parameters, determine process capability, and help improve the process. The eventual goal is the elimination of variability.

3 Statistical Basis of the Control Chart slide 25

A control chart contains three lines:

CL
Center Line
UCL
Upper Control Limit
LCL
Lower Control Limit
  • A point that plots within the limits → process in control → no action necessary.
  • A point that plots outside the limits → evidence the process is out of control → investigation and corrective action to find and eliminate the assignable cause(s).
⚠ Subtle but important

Even if all points plot inside the limits, if they behave in a systematic or nonrandom manner, this could indicate the process is out of control.

4 Choice of Control Limits — Type I & II Errors slides 35–36

Specifying the control limits is one of the critical decisions in designing a control chart — a trade-off between two errors:

ChangeType I error (false alarm)Type II error (miss a real shift)
Move limits farther from CLdecreases ▼increases ▲
Move limits closer to CLincreases ▲decreases ▼
Definitions

Type I (α): a point exceeds the limits and signals out of control when no assignable cause is present (false alarm).
Type II (β): a point falls inside the limits when the process is really out of control.

  • 3-Sigma Control Limits: probability of type I error = 0.0027.
  • Probability Limits: the type I error probability is chosen directly (e.g. 0.001 gives 3.09-sigma limits).
  • Warning Limits: typically selected as 2-sigma limits.
📊 Example values (slide 36)

With CL = 1.5 and σx̄ ≈ 0.0671:

UWL (+2σ, upper warning)1.6342
CL1.5000
LWL (−2σ, lower warning)1.3658
LCL (−3σ)1.2487
3-sigma limits UCL = CL + 3σ  ·  LCL = CL − 3σ  ·  (Warning = CL ± 2σ)

5 Sample Size, Sampling Frequency & ARL slides 37–40

  • Larger samples make it easier to detect small shifts.
  • Choice between small samples at short intervals vs. larger samples at longer intervals.
  • Current industry practice favors smaller, more frequent samples.

Average Run Length (ARL)

The ARL is the average number of points that must be plotted before a point indicates an out-of-control condition. For any Shewhart chart (uncorrelated observations):

Eq 5.2 ARL = 1p (5.2)

where p = probability that any point exceeds the control limits.

📊 In-control ARL (slides 39–40)

For an x̄ chart with 3-sigma limits, in control p = 0.0027:

ARL0 = 10.0027 = 370

So a false alarm occurs about every 370 samples on average. The run length is geometric, with a large standard deviation:

σRL = √(1 − p)p ≈ 370

The 10th and 50th percentiles are 38 and 256 (≈10% of the time the run length ≤ 38; 50% of the time ≤ 256).

6 Average Time to Signal (ATS) & Detection slides 41–44

If samples are taken at fixed intervals h hours apart, performance can be expressed as the average time to signal:

Eq 5.3 ATS = ARL × h (5.3)

In control, sampling every hour → a false alarm about every 370 hours.

⚠ The meaning of p depends on the state

In control: p = α = 0.0027 (false-alarm probability) → gives ARL₀.
Out of control: p = 1 − β (probability of detecting the shift) → gives ARL₁.

📊 Detecting a shift — n = 5 (slide 42)

The mean shifts to 1.725 microns. From the OC curve, the probability of x̄ falling between the limits ≈ 0.65 (= β), so p = 1 − 0.65 = 0.35:

ARL1 = 10.35 = 2.86  →  ATS = 2.86 × 1 = 2.86 hr
📊 Faster detection (slides 43–44)

(a) Sample more often: every half hour → ATS = 2.86 × ½ = 1.43 hr.

(b) Larger sample: n = 10 → P(between) ≈ 0.1 (β), p = 0.9 → ARL₁ = 1/0.9 = 1.11 → ATS = 1.11 hr.

A larger sample detects the shift about twice as fast. Two equivalent designs:

7 Rational Subgroups slide 45

Rational Subgroups

Subgroups or samples should be selected so that — if assignable causes are present — the chance for differences between subgroups is maximized, while the chance for differences due to assignable causes within a subgroup is minimized.

Two general approaches for constructing rational subgroups:

① Units produced at the same time (consecutive units)

Primary purpose: detect process shifts (a "snapshot" of the process).

② A random sample representative of all units produced since the last sample

Often used to make decisions about acceptance of product; effective at detecting shifts out and back between samples.

⚠ Caution

With approach ②, care is needed: you can make almost any process appear to be in statistical control simply by stretching out the interval between observations in the sample.

★ Quick Review

  • Magnificent seven: Histogram · Check sheet · Pareto · Cause-and-effect · Defect concentration · Scatter · Control chart (most sophisticated).
  • Chance causes (natural, unavoidable) → in control · Assignable causes (machines / operator / material) → out of control.
  • Chart lines: CL · UCL · LCL; inside = no action, outside = investigate; nonrandom pattern inside the limits is also a signal.
  • Wider limits ↓ type I & ↑ type II; narrower the opposite. (3σ → α = 0.0027.)
  • ARL = 1/p · ARL₀ = 370 · ATS = ARL·h · ARL₁ = 1/(1−β); larger n ⟹ faster detection.
  • Rational subgroups: maximize between-subgroup difference, minimize within-subgroup difference.
